Studio plan

The third mode beside Read and Work. Studio turns recordings into well-made books, and books into full-cast audiobooks, entirely on the user's computer.

01. What Studio is

Studio is a production desk. Every piece of work is a production: one manuscript, its structure, its cast, its audio takes and its exports, kept together in one folder. Two workflows feed the same production, so they reinforce each other: a produced audiobook automatically has a read-along book, and a transcribed lecture can be narrated again or typeset.

Book to audiobook

Pick a book or document. Studio finds the chapters, separates narration from dialogue, finds the characters, says who speaks each line, and suggests a voice for each character. The user reviews, casts and renders. Lines can be regenerated one at a time until they sound right. Exports: an audiobook (M4B, MP3 and more), the book (EPUB, PDF, DOCX and more) and a read-along edition where the text highlights as the audio plays.

Recording to book

Drop an audiobook, lecture, podcast, interview or voice memo. Studio transcribes it with timings, finds chapters and speakers, builds paragraphs, and typesets it like a professional book. Every word stays linked to the moment it was spoken, so the book can be exported as a read-along edition with the original audio.

1 · ManuscriptStructure, cleanup, spoken text, pronunciation.
2 · CastNarration and dialogue, characters, who says what.
3 · VoicesRecommendations, auditions, designed voices.
4 · RenderBatched by engine and voice, resumable.
5 · ReviewTakes, regenerate, approve, automatic checks.
6 · MasterLoudness, pauses, room tone, quality report.
7 · ExportAudiobook, book, read-along, store profiles.

Where it lives: the mode switch becomes Read · Work · Studio. Ctrl+Shift+W cycles through the three modes (Ctrl+Shift+S is already strikethrough in notes). Studio uses the same layout rules as the rest of the app: both side panels collapse independently and the inspector tabs are contextual. AGENTS.md lists Studio and speaker attribution as later scope; this plan is that later scope, and AGENTS.md is updated once the plan is approved. Voice cloning and capture stay later.

The one restriction: every export carries a small "Made with Origami Reader" credit with the crane, and every audiobook says "Made with Origami Reader" in its opening credits. Section 07 shows how to place it so stores accept the files, and how it doubles as the AI disclosure the law now asks for. Everything else stays free and unlocked, as the support dialog already promises.

Why this can stand out: the research found no tool that turns an audiobook into a formatted ebook, and no narration tool that exports a read-along EPUB from its own render. ElevenLabs Studio is the benchmark for casting and takes, but it is online, paid by credits and limits regenerations. Origami can do the same work locally, privately, with unlimited takes, in French and English from day one, and produce the book, the audiobook and the read-along edition from one production.

03. Measured on this PC

The plan's central speed question: can a whole book be narrated with studio voices in reasonable time? These are real runs of Qwen3-TTS on the development PC, using one of the Qwen-designed voices and eight novel-style sentences (about 38 seconds of audio). Your "one character at a time" idea is right. Qwen3-TTS keeps one model loaded for every voice, so switching voices is already instant. The gains come from two other places. Grouping by engine means a model (Qwen, the voice designer, a second engine, Kokoro) is loaded once instead of swapping back and forth. Grouping by voice is what makes batching possible: lines of one voice share the same prepared reference, and batching scales almost linearly (2.18, then 0.65, then 0.34). The renderer groups by engine, then by voice, across a window of a few chapters, so the chapter you are reviewing finishes first.

Update after phase 0: a batched version of the fast path (CUDA graphs captured for 8 lines of one voice) runs at 10.6 times real time with the same word accuracy, and with a batch of one it produces exactly the same audio as today. A 10-hour book now renders in about an hour on this PC. Details in STUDIO-PHASE0.md.

Laptops without NVIDIA: Qwen voices run 3.4 to 5.5 times slower than real time on 4 threads of this desktop processor, and a laptop is slower still. A whole book in studio voices would take days. Laptops therefore get light voices for full books (Kokoro and similar engines run faster than real time on a processor), studio voices for auditions and short pieces, and the option to move a production to a stronger computer for the final render.

Totals assume a 10-hour book is about 90,000 to 100,000 words and exclude retakes and automatic checks, which add roughly 10 to 25%. Long-input quality falls with these models, so render units stay at one to three sentences. Published figures for comparison: faster-qwen3-tts reports 1.8 to 2.3 times faster than real time on an RTX 4060 laptop GPU. Analysis and transcription times in later sections are estimates from published benchmarks, not yet measured here.

4.1 Manuscript

  • Start from the library. Every format Origami already imports works (EPUB, PDF, DOCX, Markdown, TXT, pasted text). A production takes its own versioned copy, so editing the manuscript never changes the reading copy, and a newer source can be merged in later.
  • Structure. Chapters from the EPUB contents or DOCX headings, font-size cues for PDFs. Front and back matter (title page, copyright, dedication, contents, acknowledgements) is detected and can be spoken, skipped or moved to the credits. Scene breaks become pauses and ornaments.
  • Spoken text. The existing skip rules and spoken versions apply: footnote markers, citations, URLs, running heads. Footnotes can be skipped, read at the end of the paragraph or read at the end of the chapter. Numbers, dates, currency, Roman numerals and abbreviations are expanded per language, because local models handle them badly.
  • Pronunciation. The existing dictionary (scopes: this book, this voice, everywhere) gains automatic suggestions: character names, rare words and words in another language are listed with "hear every occurrence" and a respelling field.
  • Text fidelity lock. The manuscript is the only source of truth. Analysis produces labels attached to character offsets. Any model output that would change the words is rejected by a word-level comparison, and the final render is checked so every sentence is spoken exactly once.

4.2 Cast: who says what

  1. Rules pass, instant and offline. A quote reader for each language: English curly quotes, French guillemets and dialogue dashes, German „ " and » «, Spanish dashes. It separates narration, dialogue, thoughts (italics, verbs such as "pensa-t-il") and letters or documents (indented blocks, salutations, signatures). Speech verbs in both word orders ("said Marie", "dit-il", "répondit Marie") attribute explicit quotes. Turn alternation, names in the vocative (the listener, not the speaker) and action beats ("Marie put down her cup.") handle many of the rest.
  2. Character list. Names are found and grouped with their aliases (titles, first name and surname, "Mr. Darcy", "Darcy", "Fitzwilliam"). The local model confirms only the uncertain merges. Each character keeps a stable ID.
  3. Model pass for uncertain quotes only. The local model sees about one to four thousand words of context, the characters active in the scene and the already-settled lines as anchors. It must answer in a fixed format: speaker ID, type of cue and confidence. Three votes are taken only when the answer is close.
  4. Character profiles. For each character, the passages that describe them are gathered, and the model suggests gender presentation, age range, possible accent or origin, personality and role, each with the evidence shown. Accent and origin are flagged as suggestions, because published tests show they are often wrong.
  5. Delivery suggestions. Speech verbs and punctuation give a first guess ("murmured", "hissed", "!", "..."), then the model picks from a fixed list (calm, warm, happy, sad, angry, afraid, whispered, shouted, sarcastic, tender, tired, urgent) with an intensity.

Review that is fast

  • A character panel with line counts, first appearance, aliases and traits. Merge, split, rename, delete, and mark as minor (minor characters share a few background voices).
  • A queue of uncertain lines. Number keys assign the top characters, N assigns the narrator, Enter confirms. Most books need a few minutes of this, not hours.
  • Corrections spread: fixing one line re-solves its dialogue run, merging aliases relabels the whole book, correcting a character's gender re-solves the "she said" lines around them. Manual choices are locked and never overwritten by a new analysis.
  • First-person books: "I" is linked to the narrator character, who can keep one voice for narration and dialogue or use a slight variant.
  • "Use one narrator" plays or renders everything in the narrator's voice without losing the cast.

4.3 Voices: casting with recommendations

  • Top three suggestions per character, with reasons: "older, British, warm; nobody else in chapter 3 uses it". Scores compare each character's profile with the voice catalog's labels (gender, age, accent, styles), weighted by how much the character speaks.
  • Distinct voices where it matters. Characters who talk to each other get voices that sound far apart. Each voice gets a voice fingerprint once, and the casting avoids giving close fingerprints to characters who share scenes.
  • Auditions on their own lines. Each suggestion is previewed on two or three of that character's real lines, including the narrator-to-character transition.
  • Design a voice for a character. The existing voice designer (Qwen3-TTS VoiceDesign) opens with a description written from the character's traits and the book's own words ("an old sea captain, gravelly, unhurried"). Three takes, pick one, and it becomes that character's fixed reference voice. This is fully local and is the most premium part of casting.
  • Consistency. Each character always renders from the same reference clip and transcript. Generated audio is never fed back as the next reference, because that drifts.
  • Cloning real people stays later scope. When it arrives it gets a consent flow: only "my voice" or a person who consented in writing, a live-read consent sentence, and labels in the exported files.

Mood references: for a main character, the designer can also make "the same voice, angry" or "the same voice, whispering" from the same description and seed. A line marked angry then renders from the angry reference. This keeps timbre close while changing delivery, and needs a short test before it is promised.

4.5 Render

  • Units: one to three sentences (about 300 characters at most), split at sentence boundaries, never inside a word. Very long sentences split at semicolons, colons or commas.
  • Scheduling: by engine, then by voice in batches sized to free graphics memory, across a window of chapters, with the chapter under review first. Quick auditions use the low-latency streaming path; bulk rendering uses batches.
  • Durable: every finished take is written to disk at once. Closing the app, a crash or a full disk loses at most the batch in progress. Rendering resumes where it stopped and shows a time estimate from measured speed.
  • Polite: reading aloud and artwork pause while rendering, using the existing job leases. "Render overnight" keeps the PC awake; on a laptop, rendering pauses on battery unless allowed.
  • Cache identity: a take is reused only if the spoken text, pronunciations, voice reference, model, settings, delivery and seed all match, as the first-release plan already requires.

4.6 Review and repair

  • Listen chapter by chapter with the same highlighting as Read mode.
  • Every line keeps its takes: play, compare, restore, regenerate, regenerate with a change (delivery, pace, pronunciation, spoken text), approve and lock.
  • Automatic checks after every take: the take is transcribed and compared with its text (missing or extra words, loops, cut endings), timed against the voice's normal pace, checked for long silences and clipping, and compared with the character's voice fingerprint for drift. Failed takes retry automatically with a new seed up to a set limit; the best one is kept and the rest are flagged.
  • A chapter waveform to adjust gaps (paragraph, dialogue turn, scene, chapter) and trim the start or end of a take.
  • Editing the manuscript compares old and new text: only changed lines become stale, approved takes elsewhere stay.
  • One review list: failed checks, uncertain speakers, unapproved lines, notes. "Approve chapter" when it is clean.

4.7 Master

  • Takes are matched for loudness, then each chapter is levelled to the chosen profile. The default follows ACX-style values, the de facto industry bar: RMS between -23 and -18 dB, peaks at -3 dB or lower, noise floor at -60 dB or lower, room tone at the start and end of each file.
  • Gaps are assembled from the pause rules; crossfades only where needed.
  • A quality report per chapter shows pass or fail for every value.
  • Later: an optional ambience bed from Origami's scene sounds, lowered automatically under speech.

4.8 Export

Audiobook

M4B with chapters, cover and metadata. MP3 per chapter in store layout (opening credits, chapters, closing credits, retail sample under 5 minutes). Opus, FLAC, WAV, ZIP. Square cover at 2400 × 2400.

Book

EPUB 3.3, accessible and validated. Print PDF for KDP and IngramSpark trims, screen PDF, DOCX (book layout or standard manuscript), Markdown, HTML, plain text.

Read-along

EPUB 3 with Media Overlays: the text highlights as the audio plays (Thorium, BookFusion, Storyteller apps; Apple Books only for fixed layout). Origami's own Read mode plays it too. SRT, WebVTT and LRC from the same timings.

  • Store profiles set the format, audio values and disclosure wording: Google Play, Kobo, Storytel, Spotify (to be confirmed), Libro.fm, direct sale and personal listening. ACX/Audible, Apple Books and INaudio are listed as not accepting local AI narration, with the date checked.
  • Every export is a snapshot of approved content, so edits during a long export never mix two editions. Exports are user-owned files: clearing takes or deleting a production never removes them.
  • A production can be packed as one file to continue on another computer, for example to render on a friend's stronger PC.

05. Recording to book

  1. Import. MP3, M4A, M4B, AAC, WAV, FLAC, Ogg, Opus, WebM and the audio track of MP4 videos, one file or a folder of chapter files. DRM-protected files (Audible AA, AAX and AAXC, Apple FairPlay, encrypted MP4, library loan files) are detected and refused with a neutral message: "This file is copy-protected and cannot be imported." No removal tools are linked or explained.
  2. Chapters. Embedded chapter markers first (M4B chapter lists, MP3 chapter frames), then one file per chapter, then long silences combined with spoken "Chapter N" or "Chapitre N".
  3. Clean audio (optional). DeepFilterNet noise reduction for noisy lectures and phone recordings, never applied by default to clean audiobooks.
  4. Transcription. Voice activity detection, then Parakeet v3 for its 25 European languages, Whisper large-v3-turbo for others. Every word keeps its time and confidence.
  5. Speakers. For interviews, podcasts and plays: speaker separation, then the user names each speaker from short samples ("Speaker 2 is Marie"). Audiobooks skip this.
  6. Book structure. Paragraphs from pauses and speaker changes, then an optional tidy-up by the local model that may only add paragraph breaks, punctuation and capitals, verified word by word. Lecture mode can remove fillers ("um", "euh"), again deletions only. Opening and closing credits of commercial audiobooks can be removed from the text.
  7. Transcript editor. Click any word to hear it. Low-confidence words are underlined and listed in a review queue. Find and replace, speaker relabelling, headings.
  8. Typeset and export. The same book design and exports as section 06. The read-along edition uses the original recording and the transcription timings, so no alignment is needed.

Later: "I also have the ebook" aligns the user's own ebook to their own audiobook (anchors plus CTC alignment with omniASR-CTC) to make a read-along edition with the publisher's exact text. And "Narrate this manuscript" sends a transcript into the book-to-audiobook workflow.

A short reminder appears when importing someone else's audiobook: a transcript for personal use is fine where private copying allows it; publishing it needs the rights holder's permission.

06. Book design

  • Themes (six to ten): Classic, Modern, Literary, Elegant, Large print and more. Each sets fonts, the chapter opener (number as "3", "Chapter Three" or "III", sunk opening, ornament), a drop cap or small-capital lead-in, the scene-break ornament, running heads and page numbers.
  • Fonts: open-licensed book faces with full French support: EB Garamond, Literata, Source Serif 4, Crimson Pro, Alegreya, Cormorant, Libertinus. About 1 to 2 MB each; checked for small capitals and the special spaces French needs before bundling.
  • Typography by language, automatic: English curly quotes and ellipses. French guillemets with narrow no-break spaces, spaces before ; : ! ?, dialogue dashes for each change of speaker, French hyphenation. Widow and orphan control, at most two hyphenated lines in a row, optimized line breaking.
  • Print: 5 × 8, 5.5 × 8.5, 6 × 9, A5 and more. Chapters open on a right-hand page, no folio on openers, roman numerals in the front matter. The inside margin grows with page count as KDP requires; bleed on request. One click makes a large-print edition.
  • Front and back matter: half-title, title page, copyright page (ISBN per format, AI narration line when there is audio), dedication, epigraph, contents, acknowledgements, about the author, also by.
  • Live preview of page spreads rendered by Typst, and of the EPUB in Origami's own reader.
  • Covers: the existing cover generator or an imported cover, plus an automatic square audiobook cover laid out from it.
  • Safety: the book's text is passed to a fixed Typst template as data and is never turned into Typst code, so nothing inside an imported book can run.

07. The "Made with Origami Reader" mark

Your requirement: a small "Made with Origami Reader" with a tiny crane at the bottom right of documents, and a spoken "Made with Origami Reader" at the start of audiobooks. The research changes where it should go, not whether.

Documents

  • Recommended: crane plus "Made with Origami Reader" at the bottom right of the copyright page (or of the last page if there is none). This is where production credits belong; Vellum's optional badge uses the same place and books with it are on every store.
  • EPUB: reflowable ebooks have no fixed page corners, so the mark goes on the copyright page and on a short closing colophon page, right-aligned.
  • Every page instead? Possible in PDF and Word, but Apple's rules forbid advertising inside books, and a logo on every page reads as a watermark or an ad. This is decision 1.
  • A small grayscale vector crane is drawn for print (about 4 mm), beside the existing lavender crane for screens. Localized: "Réalisé avec Origami Reader" in French books.

Audiobooks

  • Recommended spoken credit: "[Title], by [Author]. Narrated by digital voices. Made with Origami Reader."
  • French: "[Titre], de [Auteur]. Lu par des voix numériques. Réalisé avec Origami Reader."
  • It sits inside the opening credits, where stores expect the title, author and narrator, so it reads as a production credit and not as an ad. It also serves as the AI disclosure that Spotify, Storytel and the EU rules ask for.
  • No web address, slogan or music sting: those are what stores reject.

Machine-readable labels (the law, not only branding)

  • Metadata in every file: MP3 and M4B encoder tags and an IPTC "AI-generated" source type, EPUB generator and producer fields, PDF XMP creator tool and source type, Word application name. Each export gets a unique ID.
  • Inaudible watermark with AudioSeal (MIT): a 16-bit export ID that survives MP3 and AAC compression in independent tests. Together with metadata this gives the two layers the EU Code of Practice describes.
  • "Verify a file" in Studio: drop any audio file to see whether it carries an Origami watermark. This is the free detection tool the rules expect.
  • Later: signed provenance (C2PA) for MP3, M4A, WAV, FLAC and PDF.

License wording

APP-LICENSE.md gains a Studio clause, shown once at the first export with an accept button, and a French version (required in Quebec for standard-form contracts). Draft:

Origami Reader exports. Origami Reader adds a credit to what you export: in documents, the words "Made with Origami Reader" and the crane logo on the copyright page (or the last page if there is none) at the size the app sets; in audiobooks, a spoken "Made with Origami Reader" in the opening credits; and metadata labels and an inaudible watermark stating that the voices were generated with AI. You may publish, share and sell your exports. You may not remove, cover, crop, shrink, edit out, re-record over or otherwise hide the credit, the labels or the watermark, or have someone else do so, in an export or any copy, edition or format made from it. Any exception must be in writing from Sov3ryn. This applies only to files made with Origami Reader and does not limit rights given by the licenses of included components.

Realistically this is a contract term: it is enforced by ending the license, as the current terms already do. The mark is added by Origami's own export code, never by the GPL light reader. Apache and MIT model licenses place no limits on outputs, so the clause does not conflict with them.

11. Architecture

A production on disk

Studio/<production id>/ production.sqlite manuscript versions, chapters, segments, characters, assignments, deliveries, takes, checks, notes, history takes/ one FLAC file per take (24 kHz mono, about 0.5 MB a minute) sources/ imported recordings (recording-to-book productions) cache/ voice prompts, fingerprints, previews (safe to delete) Exports go where the user chooses and are never deleted by Studio.

A separate small database per production keeps the main library fast, makes productions portable and lets a 50,000-line book with 100,000 takes stay responsive. A typical full-cast production with retakes uses about 0.5 to 1.5 GB; Studio shows the size and can remove unused takes while keeping approved ones and every export.

Core records

  • Segment: a span of the manuscript (block, start, end), its kind (narration, dialogue, thought, letter, heading, credit), speaker, confidence, where the label came from (rule, model, person) and whether it is locked.
  • Character: stable ID, name, aliases, traits with their evidence, colour and icon, voice, delivery defaults.
  • Render unit and take: the exact spoken text after pronunciation and spoken edits, its cache key, and each take's file, length, seed, model, settings, check results and status.
  • Recording side: source files with hashes and chapters, words with times, confidence and speaker, linked to manuscript blocks.
  • Stable IDs such as c03-s042 drive EPUB anchors, read-along entries, subtitles and audio chapter marks, so every format stays in sync.

Processes

  • Rust core: owns the production databases, the durable job queue (leases, resume, cancel, one heavy GPU job at a time), audio decoding, mastering, encoding through the OS, tags, Typst, EPUB and Word.
  • Speech worker: the existing persistent Python worker gains batch requests (several lines of one voice per call), explicit seeds and per-take results. The current reading protocol keeps working.
  • Language model: llama-server as a sidecar, started only while analysis runs, answers checked against a schema.
  • ONNX in-process: transcription, voice activity, speaker embeddings, watermark, noise reduction, as Whisper and embeddings already do.
  • FFmpeg (LGPL): a separate program only where the OS has no encoder.

Rules carried over

  • Imported content is data: no scripts run, no external resources are fetched, book text never becomes template code.
  • Manual exports are user-owned and survive every cleanup.
  • Productions made from vault books are not offered in the first Studio phases (a clear message explains why); encrypted takes come later.
  • Every new string goes through translation, French first. No em dashes in authored text.
  • macOS and Linux are designed for but not claimed until run there.

13. Quality checks

  • Fidelity: for every render, the concatenated spoken text equals the manuscript's spoken text; an automated test fails on any dropped, doubled or invented sentence.
  • Attribution: accuracy reported separately for explicit, pronoun-tagged and untagged quotes, in English and French, for rules only and each model size. Target before review: at least 95% on explicit quotes and about 85% overall in English with the 9B model.
  • Durability: kill the app mid-render, fill the disk, unplug a laptop: completed takes survive and rendering resumes.
  • Exports: EPUBCheck with zero errors, DAISY Ace for accessibility, veraPDF for PDF/A and PDF/UA (in CI only, never shipped), audio values checked with ebur128, chapters read back from M4B and MP3, read-along tested in Thorium and Origami.
  • Marking: an automated test opens every export type and finds the visible credit, the metadata and, for audio, the watermark after re-encoding.
  • Licenses: cargo-deny and an npm license allowlist in CI; third-party notices updated with every new component.
  • Performance: real-time factor, peak memory and time to first audio published per engine and device, measured, not claimed.
